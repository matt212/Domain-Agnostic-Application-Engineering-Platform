# 2. DERIVE AUTHORIZATION FOR EACH ACTOR

After internally establishing an actor's role and responsibilities, analyze
that actor individually using the original business discovery data.

For each actor, internally ask and answer the relevant questions below.
Use the answers to derive permissions; do not output the questions themselves.

A. What_They_Can_See
- Which discovered business objects and information does the actor need
  to see to perform their responsibilities?
- What records, attributes, or outcomes are relevant to the actor?
- Is access limited by an established relationship, assignment, ownership,
  lifecycle state, or operating condition?
- Does the evidence justify full-object access or a narrower scope?

B. What_They_Can_Create
- Which business objects or records does the actor reasonably need to create?
- Does the actor create the object, submit a request, initiate a process,
  or merely provide information?
- Is the permission justified by the actor's actual responsibilities?

C. What_They_Can_Change
- Which existing objects, records, attributes, assignments, or states may
  the actor reasonably need to change?
- Does the actor perform the change, approve it, request it, or report
  a condition for another actor or system to handle?
- What lifecycle conditions or dependencies constrain the change?

D. What_They_Can_Remove
- Is the actor justified in cancelling, revoking, deactivating, reversing,
  archiving, or deleting a particular object or record?
- What is affected, and does the actor have the relevant responsibility
  or authority?
- Distinguish these actions according to their actual business meaning.

E. Operations_They_Can_Run
- Which meaningful business operations can the actor initiate, coordinate,
  validate, execute, approve, or record?
- What objects and outcomes do those operations affect?
- Does the actor perform the operation or merely participate in it?
- Are other actors, decisions, systems, or lifecycle conditions involved?

# 3. VALIDATE PERMISSIONS INTERNALLY

For every material permission, internally check:

- What evidence or responsibility justifies this permission?
- Does the actor need this access or action to fulfil the identified role?
- Does participation in an activity actually authorize the proposed action?
- Is the actor initiating, approving, executing, or recording the action?
- Could the action conflict with another responsibility, object relationship,
  lifecycle dependency, or established business rule?
- Does the permission depend on an unresolved condition or operating model?
- Is there a reasonable alternative interpretation that changes its scope?

Correct a conclusion when the supplied evidence supports the correction.

Retain reasonable business-specific inferences when explicit permission
details are absent. State conditions when a permission depends on a specific
condition or operating model. Preserve material uncertainty when a conclusion
cannot reasonably be derived.

These questions are for internal analysis. Do not ask the user or business
owner to answer them. Do not output a separate question list or reasoning
transcript.

# 4. AUTHORIZATION BOUNDARIES

- Distinguish business responsibility, application permission, external
  authority, and automated system activity.
- Do not infer unrestricted access from a broad role description.
- Do not infer permission to modify an object merely because the actor can
  view it or participates in a related activity.
- Do not assume that creating a record authorizes executing the activity
  represented by that record.
- Do not assume that external authority grants application-record access.
- Do not assume an actor inherits the permissions of an automated system.
- Do not invent mandatory approvals, separation-of-duty rules, financial
  limits, legal obligations, or universal industry policies.
- Use the supplied business-object names consistently.
- Do not invent undiscovered objects as established facts.
- Do not force permissions into categories when no permission is justified.

# 5. DOMAIN AND OPERATING-MODEL INDEPENDENCE

Do not assume a universal business operating model, actor taxonomy, object
ownership model, organizational hierarchy, authorization pattern, or division
of responsibilities.

Derive these from the supplied business discovery data. Where multiple
reasonable operating models exist, preserve material alternatives as
conditional rather than silently selecting one.

Apply the same reasoning method across domains without imposing
industry-specific actors, objects, processes, policies, or permissions.
