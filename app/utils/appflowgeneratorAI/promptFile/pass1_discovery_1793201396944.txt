2. PRIMARY OBJECTIVES
==================================================

For every supplied actor:

A. Discover all distinct business information systems that are
reasonably supported by the supplied business context and actor data.

B. Identify the established industry terminology applicable to each
discovered system.

C. Determine whether that actor requires any additional mandatory
capabilities to interact with or operate each identified system.

D. Populate the four capability arrays with only genuinely new,
justified additions that are not already represented in the existing
actor authorization and capability data.

E. Preserve the original actor names, existing source values, existing
permissions, and existing capability entries without modification.

Do not limit an actor to one business information system merely because
one system appears to be its primary responsibility.

Conversely, do not create additional systems simply to increase the
number of results.

==================================================
3. DISCOVER BUSINESS INFORMATION SYSTEMS
==================================================

Discover systems from the supplied context rather than relying on a
predefined list, industry template, fixed number of systems, or
hardcoded actor-to-system mapping.

Analyse the available evidence, including where present:

- Domain and niche.
- Exact actor name and operational role.
- Existing information-access capabilities.
- Existing creation capabilities.
- Existing modification capabilities.
- Existing operational capabilities.
- Business objects and information entities referenced by the
  supplied data.
- Actor journeys, activities, stages, actions, and transitions.
- Other supplied business context that directly supports the analysis.

Do not use or reference core_responsibilities as an input to infer
systems or capabilities.

Identify the distinct functional business information domains relevant
to each actor. Group related information and operations into coherent
functional systems, keeping genuinely different systems separate.

A business information system represents a coherent functional area
for managing, processing, accessing, or operating business information.

Do not confuse a system with an individual screen, UI component,
report, API endpoint, database table, business object, job title,
communication channel, or isolated action.

Do not infer that every functional system is a separately deployed
software application. The output identifies functional systems, not
physical deployment boundaries.

The same functional system may be relevant to multiple actors, but
each actor's relationship with that system must be assessed separately.

An actor may legitimately relate to several systems when the evidence
supports different information domains or operational interactions.

Distinguish between:

- Systems the actor directly interacts with.
- Systems whose information the actor is authorized to access.
- Systems in which the actor creates or modifies information.
- Systems in which the actor executes operational actions.

Do not automatically classify every indirectly related system as one
the actor can operate.

Where the supplied evidence is insufficient to justify a distinct
system, do not invent one.

==================================================
4. IDENTIFY COMMON INDUSTRY TERMINOLOGY
==================================================

For every discovered business information system, identify applicable
established industry terminology that accurately describes its
functional scope.

Use terminology appropriate to the supplied domain and niche.

Prefer recognized functional terms used in relevant business and
technology contexts. Use domain-specific terminology only where the
context justifies it.

Terminology may include a recognized system category, functional
discipline, or established industry term that describes the system's
purpose.

Do not generate a list of loosely related keywords.

Do not treat a screen name, actor title, business object, product name,
technology stack, or communication channel as an equivalent system
term unless it genuinely represents established terminology for that
system.

Do not claim that a term is an official standard, regulatory
classification, or universally accepted designation without evidence.

Avoid unnecessary synonyms, redundant terminology, and invented
industry jargon.

Each system must have its own applicable terminology. Do not
automatically assign the same generic terminology to every actor or
every system.

==================================================
5. DISCOVER NEW MANDATORY CAPABILITIES
==================================================

For each actor–system relationship, independently evaluate whether
additional capabilities are necessary within that system.

The output capability arrays represent newly discovered mandatory
capabilities, not a restatement of the actor's existing permissions.

Use the following exact semantic distinctions:

What_They_Can_See:
New mandatory information-access capabilities needed to view or
retrieve relevant information within the identified system.

What_They_Can_Create:
New mandatory capabilities needed to create relevant records,
transactions, requests, or other information within the identified
system.

What_They_Can_Change:
New mandatory capabilities needed to modify existing information or
records within the identified system.

Operations_They_Can_Run:
New mandatory operational capabilities needed to execute relevant
business operations within the identified system.

Discover capabilities from the actual business context. Do not assume
that every system requires additions in all four categories.

A capability must be both relevant to the identified system and
legitimate for the specific actor.

Use the actor's existing authorization scope to constrain the
proposed additions. Do not grant broader access, privileged actions,
approval authority, financial authority, administrative rights, or
other permissions without supporting evidence.

Do not assume that the existence of a system automatically means
every actor must access, create, modify, or operate within it.

Do not confuse a business activity with permission to perform that
activity. Ensure that every proposed capability is consistent with
the actor's evidenced authority.

==================================================
6. EXISTING-CAPABILITY DIFFERENTIAL
==================================================

The four existing actor capability arrays are the baseline against
which proposed additions must be checked.

For each actor–system relationship:

1. Read the existing capability and authorization information.
2. Determine which relevant capabilities are already represented.
3. Identify only necessary capabilities that are genuinely absent.
4. Exclude capabilities that duplicate existing permissions in
   different words.
5. Exclude capabilities that merely rephrase, split, combine, or
   summarize existing entries without adding a distinct requirement.
6. Preserve the meaning and scope of existing permissions.
7. Never return existing capability entries as new additions.

Compare meaning rather than relying only on exact string matching.
Different wording can represent the same capability.

Assess duplication in the context of the actor and system. A capability
already granted to an actor must not be reintroduced merely because
it is expressed differently in the newly discovered system context.

If an existing capability clearly covers the proposed action, do not
add it again.

If a capability is only partially covered, add a new capability only
when a distinct, mandatory and justified gap remains. Do not silently
expand the scope of an existing permission.

If no new capability is justified, return an empty array for that
category.

Empty arrays are valid and preferable to invented or redundant
capabilities.

7. EVIDENCE, INFERENCE, AND DOMAIN AGNOSTICISM
==================================================

Every system classification and capability addition must have a
defensible rationale grounded in the supplied context.

Distinguish internally between:

- Directly supported: the supplied data explicitly indicates the
  information domain, activity, or authorization.
- Reasonably inferred: the supplied context supports a necessary
  functional system or capability, even though it is not explicitly
  named.
- Unsupported: the proposed system or capability depends on
  assumptions not justified by the supplied data.

Include directly supported and reasonably inferred findings only when
they are sufficiently justified. Exclude unsupported findings.

Do not invent business objects, actors, processes, permissions,
regulatory requirements, system integrations, approval rules,
organizational structures, or operational responsibilities.

Do not impose assumptions from a particular industry on another
industry.

Do not hardcode example systems, actor names, capability lists,
industry mappings, expected system counts, or expected output counts.

Do not use a fixed checklist of business information systems as a
substitute for analysing the actual inputs.

Do not add capabilities merely because similar systems commonly
provide them in other organizations.

Industry knowledge may help interpret the supplied evidence, but it
must not replace evidence or be treated as proof that an actor has
authority to perform an action.

When an inferred system is justified but no additional capabilities
can be established, retain the system and use empty capability arrays.

Do not duplicate an actor–system relationship.

Use the exact actor name from the supplied input.

Each object's industry terminology must correspond to its identified
system.

system. Do not copy capabilities indiscriminately across systems.

Do not create an actor that does not exist in the supplied input.

system or no additional capabilities for that actor.
