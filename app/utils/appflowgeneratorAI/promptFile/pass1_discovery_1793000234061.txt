## 2. DERIVE THE FOUR AUTHORIZATION DIMENSIONS

### A. What_They_Can_See
Identify the information each actor reasonably needs for their responsibilities.
Specify the relevant object, business purpose, and appropriate scope.
Do not assume visibility grants modification authority or unrestricted access.

### B. What_They_Can_Create
Identify existing supplied business-object types the actor can reasonably
originate. Distinguish creating an object from submitting information or
triggering a process that another participant or system executes.
Do not invent new object types.

### C. What_They_Can_Change
Actively discover plausible modifications using domain knowledge; do not
simply search for explicit update permissions.

For each relevant existing object, ask:
- Given the actor's role and responsibilities, what information would they
  reasonably maintain, correct, or change?
- Which supplied objects would they plausibly modify, and for what purpose?
- Would they make the change directly, or request, submit, approve, or trigger
  a change performed by another actor or system?
- Is the modification reasonable in this business context, or does it depend
  on a material unknown policy or operating-model choice?

Include justified modifications even when update activities or exact fields
are not explicitly described. Describe changes in business language without
inventing database fields.

Distinguish direct modification from submission, request, approval, process
initiation, and automatic system updates. Do not assign direct modification
authority merely because an actor can see, create, use, or participate in
an object.

Do not force this array to be populated. Before leaving it empty, consider
the actor's domain-specific responsibilities and all relevant supplied
objects, then include any reasonably justified direct modifications.

### D. Operations_They_Can_Run
Identify meaningful business actions, decisions, and process operations
the actor can reasonably execute.

Distinguish executing an operation from requesting, approving, or initiating
it. Where a human triggers an automated change, describe the supported
initiating operation without falsely assigning direct modification authority.

Do not invent cancellation, approval, override, reassignment, settlement,
or other specific operations without contextual justification.

## 3. CONSISTENCY AND SCOPE

Evaluate each dimension independently, then cross-check:
- Every permission serves a plausible business responsibility.
- Object names match the supplied business objects exactly.
- Direct authority, delegated actions, and automated consequences remain distinct.
- Permissions are appropriately scoped and not copied across actors without
  justification.
- Reasonable domain-based inferences are included; unsupported specifics
  are excluded.
- Empty arrays reflect a genuine lack of justifiable permissions, not a
  failure to explore plausible business capabilities.

Prefer a useful, realistic authorization model over either excessive
restriction or speculative permission assignment.
